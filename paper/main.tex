\begin{abstract}
\noindent A method is described for measuring how far post-training moves a language model from the
distribution on which it was pretrained: the code length, in bits per byte, that the model assigns to text
written after its training cutoff, compared with the code length its base model assigns to the same text.
We measured this quantity for 12 public post-trained checkpoints of 0.5 to 3 billion parameters and for 12
checkpoints of our own fine-tuning runs, and compared it with benchmark accuracy. Every post-training stage
lengthened the code, by amounts from something of the order of 0.1\% for pure reinforcement learning to
43\% for mathematical training followed by reasoning distillation, and one temperature removes at most a
third of the increase. The increase measures how much post-training was applied: it tracks the
instruction-following gain, and above 15\% it was always accompanied by forgetting, but when the increase is
held fixed the recipe alone decides whether a model forgets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The entropy of a language can be estimated from the number of bits a good predictor needs to encode its
text \citep{shannon1948,shannon1951,cover1978}. A language model is such a predictor, and its code length on
unseen text has been proposed as a measure of what it has learned \citep{deletang2024compression,
huang2024compression,lotfi2024bounds}. Huang et al.\ \citep{huang2024compression} found, across 31 base models
and 12 benchmarks, that the average benchmark score is close to a linear function of the average bits per
character on held-out text, with a Pearson correlation of $-0.93$, and of about $-0.95$ within each domain.
They excluded fine-tuned models, on the ground that such a model no longer predicts arbitrary text but only
structured query and response data. Most models in use are fine-tuned, and what their code length on general
text measures has been left open.

In the present paper we measure the code length of post-trained models on general text written after their
training cutoff, relative to that of their base models on the same text. We call the relative increase the
\emph{drift}. Three questions are taken in order: whether post-training changes the code length, by how
much and at which stage; whether the change is a matter of calibration that one temperature can undo; and
whether the change predicts what post-training cost in general benchmark accuracy while ignoring what it
gained. The third question is the practical one. The drift of a checkpoint costs one forward pass over 300 kilobytes
of text, a median of 19\,s on one GPU, while one pass of our benchmark suite costs a median of 18\,min, 67
times as much. If the cheap number predicted the expensive one, a sweep of post-training recipes could be
screened at a small fraction of its evaluation cost.

The answers are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Post-training lengthened the code in all 15 comparisons we made, at every stage and on each of three
fresh corpora. Pure reinforcement learning moved it by 0.1--0.5\%, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and
preference optimisation (DPO) by 1--7\% per step, and mathematical training with reasoning distillation by
16--43\% (Section~\ref{sec:stages}).
\item A single temperature recovers at most 35\% of the increase. The rest is a change in what the model
predicts, not in how confident it is (Section~\ref{sec:temperature}).
\item Across the 12 public checkpoints the drift correlates with forgetting at $r=0.89$, but the correlation
rests on two points. The data are a threshold: every checkpoint above 15\% forgot, while 8 of the 12 lie
within the benchmark noise. The drift tracks the instruction-following gain as closely ($r=0.89$), so it is
not a one-sided meter of loss (Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}).
\item The drift is one-dimensional across token classes. A contrast between knowledge tokens and style
tokens gave a lead, $r=0.81$ with forgetting where the total drift gave $0.32$ (Section~\ref{sec:classes}).
\item Under recipe control the lead fails. With one base and three SFT recipes compared at equal drift,
forgetting is a step of about 4\% set by the recipe at the first checkpoint and constant thereafter, and
neither the total drift nor its contrast tells the recipes apart (Section~\ref{sec:matched}).
\end{enumerate}
Each experiment was pre-registered: the rules and expected values were committed before the runs, and
Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} sets them beside the outcomes. Code, dated corpora, per-token bits and benchmark
outputs are in the repository named in Appendix~\ref{app:compute}.

\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:definitions}

Let $x=x_1\cdots x_n$ be a text, encoded as a sequence of tokens, and let $q$ be a language model, which
assigns to each token a conditional probability $q(x_i\mid x_{<i})$.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{code length} of $x$ under $q$ is $L_q(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_2 q(x_i\mid x_{<i})$, in bits.
Divided by the number of UTF-8 bytes of $x$ it is the code length per byte, in bits per byte (bpb).
\end{definition}

\noindent For example, Qwen2.5-1.5B codes the 101,843 bytes of our Wikipedia corpus in 73,191 bits, or
0.719\,bpb. It is a code length in the strict sense: an arithmetic coder driven by $q$ transmits $x$ in
$L_q(x)$ bits plus a small constant \citep{deletang2024compression}.

Suppose the text was produced by a source with distribution $p$. The expected code length of one token is
then the sum of two terms \citep{shannon1948},
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[-\log_2 q(X_i\mid X_{<i})\bigr]
 = H_p(X_i\mid X_{<i}) + \mathbb{E}_p\,D\bigl(p(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\,\big\|\,q(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\bigr),
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
where $H_p$ is the conditional entropy of the source and $D$ is the Kullback--Leibler divergence of the model
from the source, both in bits. The first term depends only on the source and the second only on how far the
model is from it. It is easily seen from \eqref{eq:identity} that when a post-trained model $m$ and its base
$b$, with identical tokenizers, code the same text, the entropy of the text cancels from the difference:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_p\bigl[L_m(X)-L_b(X)\bigr]
 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_p\Bigl[D\bigl(p(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\,\big\|\,q_m(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\bigr)
 - D\bigl(p(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\,\big\|\,q_b(\cdot\mid X_{<i})\bigr)\Bigr].
\label{eq:difference}
\end{equation}
The difference of code lengths on one text therefore estimates how much further from the source of that text
the post-trained model has moved, in bits, with the unpredictability of the text removed.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{drift} of a model $m$ from its base $b$ on a corpus $x$ is $\Delta = 100\,(L_m(x)-L_b(x))/L_b(x)$,
in per cent. Because both models code the same bytes, $\Delta$ is also the relative change in bits per byte.
The drift on fresh text, $\dfresh$, is the unweighted mean of $\Delta$ over the three corpora written after
the models were released (Section~\ref{sec:corpora}).
\end{definition}

\noindent The base is the root of the model's family, the pretrained checkpoint from which it descends. Where
a checkpoint has an intermediate parent we also report the step against the parent. Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct,
for example, codes the Wikipedia corpus in 73,674 bits, a drift of $+0.66\%$ from Qwen2.5-1.5B.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{paired window bootstrap} gives the interval of a drift. Each corpus is cut into consecutive windows
of at most 1,024 tokens, never splitting a character, and each window is scored after one document-start
token. The models of a family see identical windows. Whole windows are resampled with replacement, the same
windows for both models, 5,000 times, and the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of $\Delta$ form the 95\% interval.
For $\dfresh$ the windows are resampled within each corpus separately.
\end{definition}

\noindent The interval measures the variation due to the sample of text. It does not contain the variation
between training runs, which one public checkpoint per stage cannot show.

\begin{definition}
The \emph{token-class drift} $\Delta_c$ is the drift computed on the bits of the tokens of class $c$ alone.
Each token takes the part of speech of the word under its first non-space character (spaCy
\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm}). The class KNOWLEDGE holds proper nouns, numbers, and nouns, adjectives and verbs
outside the 2,000 most frequent English words; STYLE holds function words, punctuation and whitespace; the
rest is MIDDLE. The \emph{contrast} is \KS{} $=\Delta_{\mathrm{KNOWLEDGE}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{STYLE}}$, in
percentage points (pp). A frequency-only variant replaces the classes by token ids below and above the median
count in the corpus (rare $-$ frequent).
\end{definition}

\noindent For example, OLMo-2-1B-DPO lengthens the code of its knowledge tokens by 13.20\% and that of its
style tokens by 16.20\%, a contrast of $-3.00$\pp.

The drift has the following limits, which follow from its definition.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It is not the divergence between the post-trained model and its base. Equation~\eqref{eq:difference}
compares each model with the source of the text; two models can differ widely and be equally far from it.
\item It is a property of the text as well as of the model. Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B is $+5.7\%$ from its base on
arXiv abstracts and $+33.9\%$ on Wikipedia.
\item It is measured on raw text. Scored inside both models' chat templates, Qwen3-1.7B is $+37.0\%$ from
its base, against $+16.2\%$ on raw text.
\item It is defined only between models whose tokenizers produce identical token ids on the corpus.
\item It is a distance, not a capability score, and Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that a distance which
costs capability and one which does not can be of the same size.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Controlled recipes}
\label{sec:recipes}

To separate the recipe from the dose we fine-tuned one base, OLMo-2-0425-1B, with three SFT recipes that
differ only in their data: \tulu{} (the T\"ulu 3 SFT mixture, OLMo-2's own SFT data \citep{lambert2024tulu3}),
\smoltalk{} (SmolTalk, the SFT data of SmolLM2-Instruct \citep{benallal2025smollm2}) and \mathcot{} (one
DeepSeek-R1 reasoning answer per problem from OpenR1-Math-220k \citep{openr1math2025,deepseek2025r1}). Each
recipe is a subset of 45.0 million template tokens taken in a fixed random order: 79,249 examples of mean
length 568 tokens for \tulu{}, 59,959 of 751 for \smoltalk{} and 30,512 of 1,475 for \mathcot{}. Examples
longer than 2,048 tokens were dropped, which biases \mathcot{} toward short solutions. All examples were
rendered with the OLMo-2-Instruct chat template and trained with the loss on assistant tokens only.

Training was a full fine-tune with fp32 master weights and bf16 autocast, AdamW at a peak learning rate of
$2\times10^{-5}$, 32 packed rows of 2,048 tokens per update and seed 0. The first stage ran 1,500 updates
(98.3 million token positions, 1.6 to 1.7 passes over the subset) with a cosine schedule. No recipe reached
6\% drift in that budget, and a pre-registered second stage continued each run from its final checkpoint
with a fresh optimiser and a new warm-up, until a wall-time limit stopped it at updates 3,464 to 3,489 (3.8
to 4.0 passes). The drift was scored every 20 updates against the base on the same GPU, and a checkpoint was
saved at the first scored update at or above 3\%, 6\% and 10\%. The 10\% mark was never reached. With the
final checkpoint of each stage this gives 12 checkpoints, six of them at marks.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
\label{sec:bench}

All checkpoints and bases were evaluated with lm-evaluation-harness 0.4.13 \citep{gao2024harness} in fp16 on
A10 GPUs, with greedy decoding at the harness defaults. The general score $g$ is the mean of six zero-shot
accuracies: ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag (first 2,000 documents), PIQA, WinoGrande and MMLU (first 40
questions of each of 57 subjects).

\begin{definition}
The \emph{forgetting} of a checkpoint is $F = 100\,(g_{\mathrm{base}}-g)/g_{\mathrm{base}}$, in per cent of
the base score; a positive value is a loss.
\end{definition}

\noindent The noise floor of a difference between two checkpoints is
$1.96\sqrt{\mathrm{se}_1^2+\mathrm{se}_2^2}$, from the harness's standard errors of the six-task means,
expressed in per cent of the base score. It is about 2.1\% (1.9--2.5\%) for the public checkpoints and 2.2\%
for our own. The target tasks are GSM8K (first 300 problems, five-shot, generation capped at 256 tokens) and
IFEval (first 200 prompts, prompt-level strict accuracy, capped at 1,280 tokens). The gain of a chat-tuned
model was measured with its own chat template, against its base prompted without one.

\subsection{Scoring and its checks}
\label{sec:checks}

Every text token is scored, with $-\log_2 p$ taken from the float32 log-softmax of one forward pass per
window. The document-start token is the tokenizer's beginning-of-sequence token where the tokenizer adds one
(R1-Distill only) and \texttt{<|endoftext|>} otherwise. The public checkpoints were scored with fp16 weights,
on a laptop GPU (Apple M2 Pro) for the SmolLM2 and Qwen3 pairs and on a Tesla T4 for the rest; our own runs
were scored in bf16 on the training GPU. The mechanics were checked in six ways.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The summed bits equal the library's cross-entropy loss times the token count divided by $\ln 2$, for
every model, to within $10^{-7}$ relative.
\item fp16 and fp32 agree on the first window of every model to within $1.9\times10^{-4}$ relative; nine
windows with extreme surprisals, rescored in fp32, differ by at most $5.7\times10^{-4}$.
\item Repeated forward passes are bit-identical.
\item The four models scored on both the laptop and the T4 agree to within $5\times10^{-5}$\,bpb on all 16
rows.
\item GPT-2 (124M) codes the enwik8 control at 1.243\,bpb.
\item In our own runs the bf16 base agrees with the fp16 score of the same base to within
$2.2\times10^{-4}$ relative on all four corpora.
\end{enumerate}

pipelines of the same sizes, and OLMo-2's RLVR step moved it by 0.49\%, fourteen times less than its SFT step.

tokens carry 11 to 19\% of them, and the post-trained model is worse than its base on only 43 to 63\% of the
tokens: it wins a little on easy tokens and loses a great deal on surprising ones.

3.0 times the fresh drift, the one exception being R1-Distill against its parent (0.6). The increase is
therefore not specific to text written after the cutoff, a point to which Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}
returns.

\subsection{One temperature recovers at most a third}
\label{sec:temperature}

post-trained model's logits would restore most of the code length. For the four laptop pairs we fitted the
best temperature $T^*$ on the even windows of each corpus and measured on the odd windows. The rule, stated
before the run, called the increase mostly a confidence shift if both large-drift pairs recovered at least
70\% of it, and mostly a change of belief if both recovered at most 30\%. Table~\ref{tab:temperature} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:temperature} give the outcome.

Qwen3 and R1-Distill are overconfident on fresh text. Their best temperatures are about 1.25 and 1.16, while

(per-corpus intervals 28 to 42\%) and 19\% of R1-Distill's. With each model at its own best temperature the
post-trained model still needs 10.4\% and 12.7\% more bits than its base. Under the stated rule the verdict is
mixed: Qwen3 falls in the middle band and R1-Distill in the band of changed belief. For the two light
instruction-tuned pairs the best temperature is within 0.04 of the base's, and it recovers 6 and 15\%. The
result agrees with Yang \citep{yang2026vocab}, who found on web text that a temperature together with a
vocabulary bias recovers at most 36\% of the increase in loss. One global temperature is the crudest
calibrator, and a miscalibration that depends on the context would appear here as changed belief.

this outcome the freshness of the text adds nothing we could measure.

read for chat-tuned models: SmolLM2-Instruct scores 0.0 when prompted as a base model. With chat templates the

(15.7\%), and its correlation with the drift ($r=-0.47$) cannot be interpreted. IFEval has a cap of 1,280
tokens and no reasoning model among the eight chat-tuned checkpoints. On it the drift tracks the gain as
closely as it tracks forgetting: $r=0.89$ (interval $[0.70,\,0.99]$), $\rho=0.88$. OLMo-2 SFT, DPO and RLVR,
at drifts of 7, 14 and 15\%, gain 31.5, 43.5 and 50.5 points, and Qwen3 at 16\% gains 46.5. The drift measures
how much post-training was applied. A larger dose bought more instruction following, and in these
checkpoints a dose above 15\% also cost general accuracy.

\subsection{The token-class decomposition}
\label{sec:classes}

If the drift had two components, one on names, numbers and rare words that follows lost knowledge and one on
function words and punctuation that follows changed style, the first might predict forgetting and the second
the gain. We split the drift of the same 12 checkpoints by token class (Section~\ref{sec:definitions}),
without new scoring. The pre-registered fail rule was a correlation above 0.9 between knowledge drift and
style drift, which would make the drift one-dimensional.

The rule triggers: $r(\Delta_K,\Delta_S)=0.925$, interval $[0.72,\,1.00]$. Knowledge drift ranks the 12
checkpoints exactly as total drift does ($\rho=0.671$ for both, against forgetting), and both class drifts track
the IFEval gain ($r=0.84$ and $0.81$), so neither channel hypothesis passes. Two case predictions pass:
SmolLM2-Instruct puts 63.0\% [60.1, 66.2] of its extra bits on knowledge tokens and OLMo-2-DPO 44.7\% [42.6,
46.8]. Knowledge tokens take more than their proportional share of the extra bits in 9 of the 12 checkpoints.

One secondary reading, registered without a threshold, gave a lead. The contrast \KS{} tracked forgetting
with $r=0.92$ over the 12 and $r=0.81$, interval $[0.41,\,0.98]$, without the two mathematical checkpoints,
where the total drift gave 0.32. It did not track the IFEval gain ($r=0.17$ over the eight). The
frequency-only variant, which needs no tagger, gave 0.82, 0.74 and $-0.07$. Two checkpoints at the same dose
show the reading most plainly. OLMo-2-DPO drifts 14.1\% with a contrast of $-3.00$\pp{} and forgets nothing;
Qwen3 drifts 16.2\% with a contrast of $+14.12$\pp{} and forgets 4.7\%. The lead rested on about four
informative checkpoints from different laboratories, bases and recipes, and it called for a test in which
the base and the dose are held fixed.

and by 0.91 and then 0.73\pp{} at the third, from 4.06 to 5.70\%; \smoltalk{} by 1.88\pp{} at the third, from

benchmark moved with these jumps. The repeated examples are being memorised, a process whose capacity Morris
et al.\ \citep{morris2025memorize} estimate at about 3.6 bits per parameter, and the memorisation lengthens
the code on fresh text by up to 1.9\pp{} without costing general accuracy.

One exploratory reading examined the shape of the drift across corpora. Its hypothesis, stated before it was
computed but after the benchmarks were seen, was that \mathcot{} drifts least on arXiv and most on Gutenberg;
it was not supported. The forgetting recipe does drift least on Wikipedia, at 0.70 and 0.82 of its fresh mean
at the two marks against 0.97 to 1.09 for the other recipes. This is a lead and not a result.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Three practical rules}

For post-training of models of this size the measurements support three rules.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Evaluate the first checkpoint of a new recipe.} The 4\% loss of \mathcot{} was complete at the
first checkpoint evaluated, after 1,500 updates and a drift of 2.5\%, and did not grow over the following 1,964
updates. One benchmark pass at that point would have found it. The drift did not distinguish the recipe at
any dose.
\item \emph{Conversational SFT and DPO were safe to large doses.} Our \tulu{} and \smoltalk{} runs forgot
nothing up to a drift of 9\%, and the public OLMo-2 chain of SFT, DPO and RLVR forgot nothing up to 14.7\%.
The exception is SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct, which lost 3.8\%, mostly on ARC, at 3.6\%. Our SmolTalk run on OLMo-2
did not reproduce the loss, which places its cause in the base, the DPO stage or details of the official
recipe rather than in the SmolTalk data alone.
\item \emph{Treat a drift above 20\% as an alarm.} Both checkpoints above it forgot heavily, 13.9 and 21.6\%.
The alarm is one-sided and its blind spots are large. Below 20\% the drift did not indicate forgetting:
Qwen3-1.7B forgot 4.7\% at 16.2\%, SmolLM2-Instruct 3.8\% at 3.6\% and \mathcot{} 4.1 to 4.4\% at 2.5 to 7.1\%,
while OLMo-2-DPO and -RLVR forgot nothing at 14 to 15\%. A threshold at 15\% flags the three public
checkpoints above it, all of which forgot, and raises no false alarm, but it misses SmolLM2-Instruct and was
placed after the data were seen.
\end{enumerate}
The drift costs at least 67 times less than a benchmark pass and can be logged at every checkpoint, as we did
every 20 updates. It does not replace the first benchmark pass of a recipe.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}

Huang et al.\ \citep{huang2024compression} excluded fine-tuned models from the relation between compression
and benchmark scores. Our measurements support the exclusion: after post-training the code length on general
text measures a distance from the pretrained model and not a capability. Information Capacity
\citep{yuan2025capacity} compares base and post-trained models in a figure and reports that advanced
reinforcement learning degrades compression more. In our checkpoints the reinforcement-learning steps
themselves moved the code length least, and the large increases came with distillation and mathematical
training. Yang \citep{yang2026vocab} measured the increase in loss for 14 base and derived pairs on web text,
two of them ours, and our temperature result on text written after the cutoff agrees with that study's
recovered share of at most 36\%.

Kalajdzievski \citep{kalajdzievski2024scaling} used the cross-entropy to the base model's predictions on
WikiText-103 as the measure of forgetting itself. We tested whether a quantity of this kind predicts benchmark
forgetting and found that it does so only above a threshold. Shenfeld et al.\ \citep{shenfeld2025razor} found
that the forward KL divergence from the base, measured on the inputs of the new task, predicts forgetting
across SFT and RL. Our quantity is measured on general text and did not predict forgetting within a recipe
family; the two findings concern different distributions and do not conflict. Harmon et al.\
\citep{harmon2025mapping} found low to moderate benchmark forgetting after reasoning training from base
models; in our set the distillation of reasoning traces onto a mathematical base forgot 9.0\% on its own.
Jin and Ren \citep{jin2024forgetting} predicted which upstream examples a fine-tune forgets, from the increase
of their log-perplexity; our measure is the aggregate over unseen text. Marek et al.\ \citep{marek2026forgetting}
showed that a KL penalty on pretraining or self-generated text during fine-tuning nearly removes forgetting.
Our results show that the distance is a poor gauge of forgetting at small doses; they say nothing against
using it as a regulariser, which we have not tested. InstructGPT \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt} chose its SFT
learning rate partly by a low loss on pretraining validation data. Our data suggest that such a loss tells
the size of the step taken and not what the step cost. Kostelec and Guo \citep{kostelec2026perplexity} showed
that scoring by log-likelihood can understate the gap between a distilled model and its teacher. Our general
benchmarks are scored by log-likelihood, so a loss that appears only in generation would be missed.

\subsection{Limitations}

The conclusions hold within the following limits of the measurement.
\begin{enumerate}
\item All models have between 0.5 and 3 billion parameters.
\item The benchmarks are subsampled (HellaSwag 2,000 documents, MMLU 40 questions per subject, GSM8K 300,
IFEval 200). The noise floor of one comparison is therefore about $\pm2\%$ of the base score, or about 1.3
points of accuracy, and differences in forgetting of about 1\%, such as those between \tulu{} and
\smoltalk{}, lie inside it.
\item The gain is confounded by the prompt format and the generation length. Chat-tuned models fail prompts
written for base models, and the 256-token cap truncates long reasoning, so the mathematical gain of
\mathcot{} and of R1-Distill is unmeasured.
\item The second stage of our runs was a warm restart with a fresh optimiser, and its checkpoints carry a
second warm-up in their dose. Every 6\% mark and two of the three 3\% marks come from that stage.
\item The numbers of checkpoints are small: 12 public ones from different laboratories, families and recipes,
and 12 of our own from one base and one seed. The intervals cover the sample of text, not the variation
between training runs.
\item The corpora are small, about 100\,KB each. The Gutenberg corpus consists of new transcriptions of old
books, and most of the Wikipedia corpus is new articles rather than reports of 2026 events.
\item Our recipes repeated their data up to four times, and the repetition moved the drift by up to 1.9\pp{}
without moving any benchmark.
\end{enumerate}
As the doses become smaller and the recipes more alike, the differences in forgetting become comparable with
the benchmark noise and depend more critically on the recipe and on the text that was scored.

\subsection{What would change the conclusions}

Four measurements would test these conclusions where they are weakest. The recipe test repeated at
7 billion parameters would show whether forgetting remains a step at a scale where most post-training is
done. Full benchmarks without subsampling would lower the noise floor below the differences of about 1\%
between the conversational recipes. Single-pass recipes would remove the repetition from the dose. A generation cap
long enough for reasoning chains, 2,048 tokens or more on GSM8K, would measure the gain of the
knowledge-heavy recipe and settle whether the drift is one-sided for it. A recipe whose forgetting grew
steadily with its drift, at fixed recipe and base, would overturn the reading of forgetting as a step.

\section{Summary}

We measured the code length of post-trained language models on text written after their release, relative to
their base models. Post-training lengthened the code at every stage, by about 0.1\% for pure reinforcement
learning, 1 to 7\% per step of SFT or DPO, and 16 to 43\% for distillation and mathematical training, and one
temperature removes at most 35\% of the increase. The increase is a dose of post-training. It tracks the
instruction-following gain, and above 15\% it was always accompanied by forgetting. Under recipe control,
forgetting was a step set by the recipe by the first checkpoint, independent of the drift and of its
direction across token classes. As a cheap alarm for large departures the drift is useful; as a meter of
forgetting at the doses of ordinary instruction tuning it is not.
